Spec-Driven Development in Scrum and Kanban: Where the Spec Actually Lives
Spec-Driven Development isn’t a new waterfall. See where to place the spec in the Scrum and Kanban cycle, and how to turn it into a Definition of Ready.

Everyone repeats that a spec is the opposite of agility — yet teams that write the contract before the code ship more reliably and faster than teams that don’t. Picture two sprints run by the same team, side by side. The first starts the classic way: a story, a conversation, code, review. The second starts from a contract, and an AI agent generates the implementation from it.

That distinction stopped being academic the moment you let an agent into the repository. The model executes exactly what it finds in the contract — so the quality of the spec becomes the quality of the output, and a vague prompt turns the sprint into a string of fixes. A human would have asked a follow-up question. An agent will simply build the wrong thing, with full confidence.

In a few minutes, you’ll know where to place the spec in the Agile cycle, how to turn it into a Definition of Ready — the criteria a task must meet before it enters the sprint — without sliding back into waterfall, and how to spot the moment your process starts sliding there anyway. The question of which of those two sprints shipped faster has an answer less obvious than intuition suggests.

The Single Distinction That Matters Most
The single most important thing to take from this article fits in one sentence. The real tension doesn’t run between “fast delivery” and “good spec-writing” — those two things don’t face off against each other. It runs between a frozen spec and a living one.

Spec-Driven Development, or SDD — the approach where you write a precise contract before the code exists, and an AI agent builds against it — gets mistaken for both. A frozen spec is Big Design Up Front: a complete design agreed upon before the start and defended against change by phase gates (BDUF and phase gate — freezing the entire design before implementation begins). That’s waterfall, and it’s what gives SDD its bad name. A living spec is a contract that matures inside the sprint, updated whenever the implementation teaches the team something.

The entire difference between “SDD is the new waterfall” and “SDD strengthens Agile” comes down to which side of that axis you stand on. The practical takeaway for Monday is simple: don’t dump finished specs into the Product Backlog. Keep the backlog at the level of goal and intent, and develop the contract — precise enough for the agent to build the right thing — only after the task is pulled into the sprint. That single shift decides whether AI speeds up your team or buries it in Markdown.

Let’s start by defusing the dilemma that forces a choice between speed and quality — because it’s a false choice.

The Tension Isn’t Where You Think
Back to the opening scene — two sprints, two different first steps. In the classic flow, a task starts life as a story and a conversation — only from that conversation does the code emerge. In the SDD flow, a task starts from a contract, and the agent generates the implementation from it, which the team then verifies. What changes is the starting point, not the sprint’s length — and that starting point is exactly what triggers the whole dispute.

The Manifesto Never Banned Documentation
“Plans are worthless, but planning is everything.” — Dwight D. Eisenhower, General and President of the United States

That line cuts straight to the heart of the SDD debate. The value isn’t the frozen plan-document; it’s the living planning process — and that’s the exact distinction critics miss. The objection I hear most often is “that’s just waterfall in Markdown,” and it’s a fair one — but only against a frozen spec.

The Agile Manifesto never banned documentation. “Working software over comprehensive documentation” states a priority, not an exclusion — the authors themselves added that there’s value on the right side too. Agility isn’t the absence of a contract; it’s the contract’s right to change after the build starts. So the “waterfall in Markdown” objection aims at the wrong target: at the spec’s presence, not at its being frozen.

The Axis That Actually Matters
Here’s the axis that actually matters. Waterfall resists changing the spec once implementation starts and defends it with phase gates. SDD assumes the opposite — that implementation will teach the team something, so the contract matures in short feedback loops.

The same artifact is frozen in one case, a versioned contract in the other. It isn’t the document’s presence that decides agility; it’s its capacity to evolve.

With an AI agent, that axis sharpens more than it ever has before. A controlled study on a greenfield task showed a clear speedup when working with an AI assistant. A study on a mature repository with millions of lines of code showed a real slowdown for experienced developers.

Different conditions, different results: AI speeds things up on a green field and can slow you down on mature legacy, because there the gap a human would have questioned turns into a wrong implementation. The spec is exactly the place where you close that gap before the agent starts building.

Since not every spec is equal, we need a way to tell “too little” apart from “too much” — and that’s where the three-rung ladder comes in.

Three Levels of Spec, and the Ladder You Climb
SDD isn’t one mode; it’s a ladder — and the most common disappointment comes from confusing its rungs. Birgitta Böckeler of Thoughtworks organized this mess into three levels worth keeping in mind before you reach for any tool.

Böckeler’s Three Rungs
At the lowest rung, spec-first, the spec leads the work, but the code remains the main artifact under maintenance. It’s a note precise enough for the agent to build the right thing; then it fades into the background. Above it sits spec-anchored — here the contract lives alongside the code as a versioned artifact and evolves with it. At the top is spec-as-source, where the spec IS the source of truth, and the code is a generated product treated as a disposable artifact.

Each rung raises both the maintenance cost and the tightness of coupling between the spec and the code. Most teams should stay at spec-first. Spec-anchored makes sense where multiple teams or agents share one contract — a public API, for instance. I’d leave spec-as-source to experiments and safety-critical systems, where certification requires a formal model anyway.

The problem is that the word “spec” is blurry. It can mean anything from an elaborate prompt to dozens of Markdown files, so every SDD statistic demands the question of which level it refers to. A metric from a spec-anchored project doesn’t transfer to your simple bug fix — and conflating levels is the single costliest mistake in the whole topic.

Three Proven Integration Patterns
In practice, integration takes three proven shapes. Just Enough Spec is a legacy from earlier Agile methods — specify wide, not deep, just enough to understand the shape of the solution, without drilling into every detail. Spec as Definition of Ready wires the contract into the sprint’s entry gate. And Constitution plus Spec adds a layer of fixed design principles above the spec — a constitution, a set of invariant rules that apply across every change — from which you then derive the next steps.

There’s exactly one antipattern: paying the cost of a higher rung while your problem sits at a lower one — when a simple bug spawns several stories and a dozen acceptance criteria.

Once we know HOW MUCH to specify, the question that remains is WHERE in the cycle to do it — and that’s exactly where Scrum and Kanban require real adjustments.

Where the Spec Actually Lives in the Cycle
The most common integration mistake is placing the full spec in the Product Backlog. The spec doesn’t belong in the backlog or in refinement — it matures inside the sprint.

Not the Backlog — the Sprint
Yuval Yeret puts it plainly: the team agrees on goals and context in the backlog and develops the actual contract only during the sprint, as part of doing the work. That seemingly small shift resolves the entire “how do you reconcile SDD with short cycles” dilemma. Scrum provides the container; it doesn’t micromanage the spec cycle inside it — the same way it doesn’t micromanage individual tests inside a TDD session. Placing a finished, complete contract in the backlog is a signal that the spec sits at the wrong level of abstraction, one rung too low relative to the intent.

In Scrum, the natural anchor point is the Definition of Ready. The spec already supplies what the DoR requires: acceptance criteria, scope boundaries, and edge cases. The contract fills the DoR almost naturally — on one condition: that it doesn’t turn into a stage-gate.

Definition of Ready as an Agreement, Not a Gate
This is where the real dispute starts. Mike Cohn warns that the Definition of Ready can be “a dangerous step backward toward waterfall” — a gatekeeper that blocks entry into the iteration and shifts the conversation from collaboration to a formal contract. The popular rule of three to five acceptance criteria per story is reasonable enough on its own.

But the resolution is qualitative, not procedural: the DoR works as the team’s shared agreement, not as a weapon aimed at the Product Owner. Treat it as an entry-quality criterion, not a phase gate.

Kanban has no sprint, so the commitment point takes over the DoR’s role — the moment a task stops being optional and becomes a commitment to deliver. A task crosses it only once it’s “ready to pull,” meaning there’s clarity about what needs to be built. Before that point lives Upstream Kanban — the analysis and contract-design phase ahead of the main flow, with work-in-progress limits (WIP limit — a cap on tasks at a given stage) that guard against overproducing specs that go stale before anyone implements them.

The theory sounds coherent — but the numbers and failures from real rollouts show when this model delivers and when it turns the sprint into a Markdown factory.

When It Works, and When It Kills the Sprint
The data confirms the direction, but reading it demands discipline. This isn’t a paradox — it’s a map of conditions. A spec paired with short loops improves velocity and quality where the problem is complex and touches multiple people. It gets in the way where the change is small and the SDD level is set too high.

The Success Side: Three Different Contexts, One Direction
Let’s start on the success side, keeping every number’s context intact. In a quasi-experiment comparing Scrum with a version enriched by an architectural spec (sMBSAP, Colorado State, 2023), velocity rose by roughly 19%, and defect density dropped by roughly 31%. That’s a controlled comparison of two working modes for the same type of team, not a rollout anecdote — though it’s a single project, so the result points to a direction, not a guarantee it transfers.

A similar direction shows up on a larger scale. The next generation of a certain semiconductor manufacturer’s product — despite being more complex than its predecessor — had roughly half as many defects after the team introduced a full, reviewed spec. Complexity rose, and defects still fell — a single case from one product line, but a direction consistent with the other two.

The third example comes from banking. At a global bank where more than a third of developer time went into firefighting urgent defects, that share dropped to single digits after nine months of working with behavior-driven specs — without a costly system rewrite.

Three different contexts, one direction: when the spec matched the scale of the problem, quality went up. That doesn’t mean more spec always means better — it means a well-fitted contract pays for itself in quality.

The Cost Side: When the Spec Kills the Sprint
The cost side is just as instructive. A detailed, negative experiment from a practitioner showed roughly a tenfold slowdown with a rigid SDD workflow on a small CRUD feature — because the tool generated more lines of Markdown than code, and every one of them had to be reviewed. That’s spec drift in its early stage — the gap between the spec and the actual code, once there are more documents than the team can maintain. A typical scenario described by practitioners illustrates it well: dozens of spec files scattered across multiple repositories, some describing the same service in several different ways.

The most interesting failure, though, wasn’t technical — it was procedural. SDD imposed top-down as a mandate, instead of being introduced as an experiment, turned the organization against it — complete with a petition signed by more than a thousand engineers and metric-gaming in the background. I treat this as a warning sign, not a certainty, since some details of the story remain unverified. Still, the conclusion is clear: what decides the outcome is fitting the level to the context, not the mere presence of a spec.

Since fit and measurement decide the outcome, the question left is: how do you actually pick the SDD level, and what should you NOT do.

Fit the Level to the Problem, Measure Instead of Believing
The Selection Rule in Three Sentences
The selection rule fits in three sentences. Spec-first for most of the work. Spec-anchored with contract tests in CI (automated tests that check the implementation against the spec on every merge) where multiple teams share API contracts. Spec-as-source only experimentally, or in safety-critical systems.

Keep the backlog and refinement at the level of goal, develop the contract inside the sprint, and make the DoR a quality agreement, not a gate.

Measure — Don’t Trust Perception
The biggest risk in the whole undertaking, though, is psychological, not technical. It isn’t the absence of a spec that’s most dangerous — it’s the conviction that you’re faster when the measurement says otherwise. In one study, experienced developers expected roughly a 24% speedup from AI, and the actual measurement on a mature repository showed a 19% slowdown (METR, 2025, sixteen developers, wide confidence interval). The gap between perception and metrics held steady — and that gap, not the technology itself, is the real opponent.

That’s why you roll out SDD like an experiment, not a mandate. Start tracking two or three metrics before you declare victory: cycle time, rework rate, and the ratio of code lines to Markdown lines. If the share of rework grows after the rollout, or the number of spec files bloats faster than the code, that’s a signal to pull back the batch size, not to pile on more process.

The last piece is contract ownership. The spec has to be a living, versioned artifact with a single owner — otherwise spec drift creeps back in, and with it a false sense of control. Keep a sane cap on invariant rules, too: too many fixed constraints don’t buy you more order, it buys you more places for contradictions to hide.

Summary: Which Sprint Delivered
Back to the two sprints from the opening. The one that delivered faster was the one where the spec lived inside the sprint at the right level — not the one that got a finished, frozen contract handed down from the backlog. The rest of this article is just the unpacking of that one distinction.

Five things worth carrying back to your own team:

The tension isn’t “fast versus good”; it’s “frozen spec versus living spec” — and only the frozen one deserves the waterfall accusation.
SDD is a ladder — spec-first, spec-anchored, spec-as-source — pick the rung that fits the problem, and don’t pay the cost of a higher one for the benefit of a lower one.
The spec matures in the sprint, not in the backlog — and in Kanban, at the commitment point. It feeds the DoR as a quality agreement, not a gate.
The data confirms the direction; the failures come from mismatched levels and top-down mandates — it’s a map of conditions, not a paradox.
Measure — don’t trust perception — track cycle time and rework rate before you declare a speedup.
This Week’s Move
The practical move for the coming week is concrete: shift spec-writing from refinement into the sprint, turn the DoR into the team’s working agreement, and switch on two metrics that will tell you whether AI is actually speeding up your work or just feels like it is.
